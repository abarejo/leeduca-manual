How we estimate risk and reading difficulty

Status: draft of October 2026, updated November 8, 2026. Decided (November 8, 2026): the five certainty levels are the manual’s reference classification, with P10 as the threshold for severe difficulty (DL-A). This chapter describes the criterion used by the task sheets and the prediction analyses. Translated from the Spanish version of November 27, 2026.

A single low score in one term is not enough to speak of reading difficulty. A child may have a bad day, have just arrived, or progress at a different pace and later catch up with their peers. That is why the manual distinguishes between risk, which is estimated before children read, and difficulty, which is only confirmed when reading remains low over several school years.

Two ideas from the literature support this approach:

  • Dyslexia has no single prevalence. Reading is continuously distributed, so prevalence depends on the severity cut-off chosen. Moreover, among poor readers there are more children whose reading is consistent with the rest of their achievement than children with dyslexia (Wagner et al. 2020). That is why several certainty levels are used rather than a single cut-off.
  • Screening works better when it is repeated. In preschool, a brief battery of letters, phonological awareness and rapid naming predicts end-of-Grade-1 reading well. If progress in letter knowledge is also monitored during the year, prediction improves (Catts et al. 2015). This is the logic of assessing three times per school year.

Reading in each school year

From age 6, reading in each school year combines two components:

  • Decoding: word and pseudoword reading.
  • Fluency: text reading speed.

Each component is expressed as a percentile against the normative reference sample: children from non-Preleo schools in all cohorts (2010–2013). Reading for the school year is the mean of the two.

Cohorts are pooled because they are equivalent: for the same task and time point, the median difference between a cohort and the pooled reference is d = 0.11. If a cohort departs by more than 1 SD from the others, or its spread is more than three times larger or smaller (a change in task version or an export error), it uses its own reference, and this is recorded.

Five certainty levels

Using reading in the school years from age 6 to 11, each child is assigned one of five levels. At least two school years must have been assessed, one of them from age 7 onward.

Level What it means Rule
DL-A · Severe persistent reading difficulty Reading stays very low year after year ≤ P10 in at least two school years and ≤ P25 in all years from age 7
DL-B · Persistent reading difficulty Reading stays low in most school years ≤ P25 in at least two school years and in at least half of them
TR · Transient difficulty Low in some school year, but not sustained ≤ P25 in some school year
INT · Intermediate Never low, but in the lower part of the average Never ≤ P25 and mean percentile < 40
TE · Stable typical Reading at or above the average in all school years Never ≤ P25 and mean percentile ≥ 40

DL-A and DL-B together make up persistent difficulty: the criterion against which the predictive validity of the tasks is studied. DL-A versus TE makes up the certainty sample, with the clearest cases at both extremes. It is used to check whether a task discriminates well when there is no doubt about the classification.

Across cohorts, 15% of children have a persistent difficulty (4% severe and 11% moderate). Another 20% go through a transient difficulty. The proportions are similar in Preleo and non-Preleo schools (15.6% and 14.3% persistent difficulty). This comparison is descriptive: it does not measure the effect of the program, which is studied with a different design.

Why more than one school year is needed

A child who reads poorly in one school year is quite likely to keep reading poorly the next, but not all do. The lower the score and the closer the two school years, the more stable it is.

Of the children below P10 at age 6, almost 9 in 10 are still below P25 at age 7. But at age 8 only 7 in 10 remain. One low school year is a serious signal, and even so it is not enough for a diagnosis.

What is said at each age

The same logic sets the language of the report according to age:

Age What can be said Based on
4–5 Risk (low, moderate or high) Precursors: phonological awareness, letters and rapid naming
6 Provisional difficulty Grade 1 reading below P25 or P10
7–8 Confirmed difficulty ≤ P10 at ages 7–8, repeated in the following school year
6–11 (retrospective) Certainty level (DL-A … TE) The whole reading trajectory; this is the research criterion

How well the precursors predict

With the precursors at age 5 (phonological awareness, letters and rapid naming), persistent difficulty is predicted with an area under the curve of .82, validated by leaving each cohort out. At age 4 it is .73, and with both school years together, .83. Letters and phonological awareness carry most of the weight; rapid naming adds nothing at age 5.

At age 4 the precursors are useful for monitoring, not for screening. At age 5 they already allow support to be prioritized, always as risk and not as a diagnosis. Details are in each task sheet and in a prediction article in preparation.

Universal screening: what the evidence says and where the platform fits

How many children: prevalence depends on the criterion

Figures for dyslexia in Spanish vary widely, and the main reason is the criterion used to identify it:

Source Population Criterion Prevalence
Meta-analysis in Spanish (Cubilla-Bonnetier 2024) 16 studies Variable 7.5% (5.2–10.9), with large heterogeneity
Worldwide meta-analysis (Yang et al. 2022) Primary school Variable 7.1%; similar in transparent and opaque orthographies
Uruguay (Cuadro, von Hagen, and Costa Ball 2017) 1,408 schoolchildren Several procedures on the same sample 2.2–5.3% depending on the procedure
Molina de Segura, Murcia (Carrillo Gallego et al. 2011) 1,894 children in Grades 2, 4 and 6 (all schools in the municipality) Two stages: group TECLE (−1.5 SD) and confirmation with words, pseudowords and spelling 11.8% (Grade 2: 15.2%; Grade 4: 9.3%; Grade 6: 10.9%)
Canary Islands (Jiménez et al. 2009) Primary school Teacher nomination and confirmation with PROLEC (bottom 25%) 8.6% (3.2% reading only; 5.4% reading and spelling)
Andalusia (Villegas Lirola 2023) 944,118 schoolchildren Administrative diagnosis 1.24%; diagnosis at around age 10
Málaga, LEEDUCA (Loredo et al. 2025) 691, Grades 5–6 −1 SD in lexical decision and fluency 8.5% at risk
Canary Islands (García et al. 2013) Grade 2 to Year 10 Text comprehension 20.2% with comprehension problems; 6.7% poor comprehenders

Four takeaways for the manual:

  • There is no single “true” prevalence. Reading is continuously distributed, so the figure depends on the cut-off (Wagner et al. 2020). That is why the platform does not report a prevalence, but certainty levels with their percentage in each cohort.
  • With psychometric criteria, the figure in primary school is around 9–12%. This is what results from a first group stage and an individual confirmation (Carrillo Gallego et al. 2011; Jiménez et al. 2009).
  • Schools identify late and too little. Andalusia’s 1.24%, diagnosed at around age 10, against an expected 5–8%, points to underdiagnosis. Identification in preschool and Grade 1 is exactly what the platform can contribute.
  • Comprehension is a different problem. A substantial share of poor comprehenders have no decoding difficulty. This is the reason for adding comprehension as a separate dimension (see “Pending”).

The precedent: DIS-ESP’s two-stage screening

The Molina de Segura study (Carrillo Gallego et al. 2011) is the platform’s direct antecedent. M. S. Carrillo and J. Alegría, authors of the DIS-ESP battery, applied a sequential procedure to all primary school children in one municipality:

  1. First stage. Group TECLE in the classroom. The cut-off (mean − 1.5 SD of typical readers) is equivalent to a delay of about two school years. It flagged 12.5% of the children, compared with the 6.7% that would be expected if reading were normally distributed. This indicates that the distribution has a heavier lower tail, where readers with dyslexia are concentrated.
  2. Second stage. Word and pseudoword reading and spelling tests (dictation of words with inconsistent spellings and orthographic decision) to look for the proximal cause. Of the flagged children, 96% had a deficit in the phonological mechanism, the orthographic one or both. 77% had both.

Three results from that study reappear in the platform data:

  • Delay in Grade 2 is partly transient. In Grade 2, 18% of delayed readers had only an orthographic problem (5% in Grade 6), and a quarter read pseudowords well. The authors expected many of them to recover with more practice. This is what the platform captures with the TR level (transient difficulty) as opposed to DL-A and DL-B.
  • Around 10% keep the difficulty in Grades 4 and 6, many without reaching even a Grade 2 reading level.
  • Figures depend on the procedure. With the same approach, 8.6% was found in the Canary Islands (Jiménez et al. 2009), but the first selection was made by teachers (27.9% of the sample) and the cut-off was the bottom 25% on PROLEC.

The platform turns that procedure into a continuous one: the first stage is repeated three times per school year from age 3, and the second (words, pseudowords, orthographic decision) is already in the same battery.

What accuracy to expect from screening

  • In Spanish there are screeners with good accuracy in preschool. A set of curriculum-based measures in the final year of preschool reached an AUC of .83 in autumn and of .94–.97 in winter and spring. Phonemic awareness and knowledge of letter–sound correspondences were the best single measures (Gutiérrez et al. 2020). Lexiland, a tablet-based screener for 5-year-olds, identified 90% of those who would have difficulties at the end of Grade 1, with a specificity of 76% (Zugarramurdi et al. 2022). In Catalan, Prelectocat provides cut-offs at age 5 (López-Sala et al. 2025).
  • Sensitivity tends to fall short. A meta-analysis of 30 studies of computer-adaptive screeners finds a specificity of .83 and a sensitivity of .77, below the .80 recommended for universal screening (Shen et al. 2026). Cut-offs computed with local data improve sensitivity at the cost of specificity.
  • Repeating or adding a second stage reduces false positives. Adding brief progress monitoring or a dynamic assessment reduced false positives in Grade 1 (Compton et al. 2010). A second stage only with children who do not pass the first increases specificity, but may lower sensitivity (Klingbeil et al. 2017). It works better if the second measure adds information different from the first: two highly correlated measures barely reduce false positives (Van Norman et al. 2019).

The LEEDUCA early identification guide

The group’s guide for speech and language teachers and preschool and primary teachers (Luque 2022) organizes assessment in three moments, each with its own aim:

  1. Prediction or risk assessment, in preschool, before formal reading instruction.
  2. Early identification, in the first year of learning to read (Grade 1), with at least three assessments of all children, because a single measurement is not enough.
  3. Psychoeducational assessment and diagnosis, from Grade 2, instead of “waiting to fail”.

To assess risk, it proposes four criteria, each with a judgment of mild, moderate or severe risk: family risk (with a case history and the ATLAS questionnaire), oral language (first the phonological triad: phonological awareness, rapid naming and verbal memory; then the semantic, grammatical and prosodic components), visual hypotheses and comorbidity with other neurodevelopmental disorders (DLD, ADHD, dyscalculia and developmental coordination disorder). For early identification, direct assessment of reading (phonological route, visual route, speed and comprehension) takes precedence as the criterion for intensifying instruction, even if the other criteria do not confirm the risk, and it is graded with cut-offs at P10, P20 and P30.

The platform and this manual follow that logic: universal and repeated assessment, several indicators rather than one, direct reading as the main criterion from Grade 1, and graded judgments (see Report bands (in Spanish)). What the manual adds is estimation with data: calibrated probabilities, certainty levels and a watch zone.

Where the platform fits

  • Comparable accuracy. The age-5 model (AUC .82, validated by leaving each cohort out) is within the range of published screeners in Spanish.
  • Monitoring is already built in. Three assessments per school year are equivalent to the second stage or the progress monitoring recommended in the literature. No published screener in Spanish has that monitoring at this scale.
  • A two-gate procedure. The scheme and its rationale are in The two-gate funnel (in Spanish).
  • Proposed study. Use the platform data to simulate two-stage screening: a first stage with letters and phonological awareness, and a second with the least correlated task (for example, RAN or change between terms). Compute sensitivity and specificity against persistent difficulty and against Woodcock-Muñoz.

Why this variable is useful in other analyses

The certainty level summarizes a child’s whole reading trajectory in a single variable, built with fixed rules and the same reference for all cohorts. It can therefore also serve as an outcome in other studies; for example, to compare the proportion of persistent difficulty between schools with and without a program, or to describe which severity group children with a given risk profile end up in.

Caution: the level is built from words, pseudowords and text speed. Comparing those same tasks against it from age 6 is therefore circular: it measures agreement, not predictive validity (see the reading speed task sheet).

Why this classification

Group decision (November 8, 2026). The manual adopts the five certainty levels as the reference classification of reading difficulty, with P10 as the threshold for severe difficulty (DL-A), instead of −1.5 SD (≈ P7). Three reasons:

  • It uses the trajectory, not a snapshot. Difficulty is defined over several school years, as the IDA definition requires by placing at its center difficulty that persists despite instruction (International Dyslexia Association 2025). A single assessment is not enough.
  • It separates certainty from severity. Distinguishing severe persistent (DL-A), persistent (DL-B) and transient (TR) difficulty makes it possible to study what distinguishes children who recover from those who do not, which is a central theoretical question for cumulative risk and resilience models (Catts and Petscher 2022).
  • It is less conservative without losing certainty. With P10, fewer children with severe difficulty go unidentified, and the requirement that it be repeated in at least two school years and not exceed P25 from age 7 preserves certainty.

The same logic for oral language and mathematics (proposal)

The classification does not depend on reading: it only requires one measure per school year, a cut-off and several assessed school years. The proposal is to apply it in the same way to two other domains:

Domain Measure per school year (proposed) First school years with data Purpose
Oral language Vocabulary and grammatical comprehension (see Screening two risks (in Spanish)) Ages 4–5 Distinguish weak oral language that persists from weak oral language that recovers; separate the risk of dyslexia from that of language disorder
Mathematics Arithmetic and magnitude tasks in each school year (see Numerical cognition (in Spanish)) Ages 5–8 Persistent difficulty in mathematics and its co-occurrence with reading difficulty

For the levels to be comparable across domains, the same rules are used (P10 and P25, at least two school years, one from age 7). What must be checked in each domain is that there are enough measures in each school year and that they are reliable.
