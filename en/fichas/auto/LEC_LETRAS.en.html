Theoretical framework

Letter knowledge is the ability to recognize each grapheme and associate it with its name or sound. It is the gateway to the alphabetic principle. The platform measures it in two ways: naming letters, and choosing the letter with which the name of a picture begins, which also requires isolating the first sound.

What the evidence says. Together with phonological awareness and rapid naming, it is one of the three major predictors of reading. Its weight is similar in English, Spanish and Czech (Caravolas et al. 2013). In Spanish, letter knowledge is the best predictor of simple syllable reading; phonological awareness weighs more for complex syllables (Martínez and Goikoetxea 2020). The full review of the evidence is in the letter knowledge pilot task sheet (in Spanish).

What our data show. Both tasks have very high internal consistency (α = .90 and .97). Letter naming is stable between terms (ρ = .73) and predicts low Woodcock-Muñoz fluency years later with a median AUC of .71. As a skill with a ceiling, it discriminates mainly at ages 4 and 5.

Each form (platform code) has its own norms, because the forms need not measure on the same scale.

Floor effect. At 6.1 (form 1887) P10 coincides with the worst possible score (at 6.1 (form 1887), P25 too): many children are tied at the minimum and the norms do not distinguish between them. At those time points this task cannot be used to grade difficulty.

These are norms by time point (school year × term). For dates between assessments and time points without a normative sample, study M3 proposes continuous norms on months of instruction, not on age, with relative age within the school year as an adjustment.

Forms: can they be pooled?

Forms given at the same time point are compared. They are considered equivalent if the mean difference is smaller than 0.20 SD and the SD ratio is between 0.80 and 1.25. If the same children took them, linear equating is also computed, which puts the second form on the scale of the first, together with the correlation between forms (parallel-forms reliability). If the groups differ, the difference mixes form and cohort.

For publications

The LEEDUCA platform task “Letter knowledge” (codes 1887, 535, 5431 and 67; LEEDUCA battery) assesses alphabetic knowledge. The child reads aloud the stimuli shown on the screen and the examiner records each correct response or error. It comprises 52 items. The time limit is 60 seconds. It is administered individually on a computer at school. In the 2010, 2011, 2012, 2013 and 2014 cohorts it was administered in 17 sessions, from 4 to 6 years of age. The main outcome is the efficiency (correct responses per unit of time). In our data (2021 analyses), internal consistency was α = .97 (N = 2,127, 52 items). Stability across successive administrations (median interval of 4 months) was ρ = .76 (median of 12 session pairs; range -0.06–.92). Because the task is timed, α tends to overestimate reliability; stability across administrations is the preferred estimate. Stability across time points was ρ = .40–.93. Against later poor reading comprehension, AUC = .57–.65.
