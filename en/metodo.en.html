How each task sheet is built

Translated from the Spanish version of November 27, 2026.

Task sheets follow the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, and National Council on Measurement in Education 2014) and the argument-based approach to validity (Kane 2013): each use proposed for a score (identifying risk, monitoring progress, comparing with norms) must be backed by specific evidence.

Sections of a task sheet

Section What it contains
Description What the child does, items, forms, task and session codes, main measure
Theoretical framework What the task measures and what the literature says about its value
Sample N by measure, time point and cohort; Preleo condition; schools; platform; data cleaning
Descriptive statistics Mean, SD, percentiles, skewness, floor and ceiling effects
Reliability Test–retest and agreement between terms (Bland-Altman), alternate forms, internal consistency (α/ω) when item-level responses are available, reliable change
Validity Concurrent, discriminant, predictive (AUC against later reading difficulty) and incremental
Norms Percentiles by time point in the reference sample; precision of the percentile
Variables that influence scores Relative age, school socioeconomic index (SES), sex, Preleo, school; differential prediction
Studies it opens up Methodological, theoretical and applied questions the task makes it possible to answer
Pending issues Data errors found and what is missing

What backs each proposal

When a task sheet proposes a use or a decision, it states what it rests on:

  • [E] Published evidence: peer-reviewed studies that support the measure or the criterion.
  • [D] Our own data: analyses of the LEEDUCA data shown in the task sheet.
  • [U] Utility criterion: a proposal by the group based on its clinical, educational or research value, still without direct evidence.

Reference sample and Preleo

Preleo is a prevention program that some schools delivered in preschool. So that the norms describe development without intervention, the main reference is the normative reference sample: children from schools that did not deliver Preleo to their cohort. In analyses of the program’s effect, the terms Preleo group and comparison group are used. “Control group” is not used because schools were not randomly assigned. Tables show the internal codes SIN (non-Preleo) and CON (Preleo).

The innovations

Besides the classical indicators, task sheets include analyses designed for the real use of the platform:

  • Agreement between terms and reliable change: how much a child has to change to claim that they have really improved.
  • Precision of the percentile: the band within which a child’s percentile lies with 90% confidence.
  • Age in months: whether it is better to norm by exact age and not only by school year and term (see Norms by school year or by term).
  • Validity without circularity: when the reading difficulty criterion contains the measure itself, another criterion is sought.
  • Differential prediction: whether the same cut-off works equally well for boys and girls or for schools of different socioeconomic status.
  • Understandable units: for example, words per minute and the minutes a child takes to read 1,000 words.

Reproducibility

Computation is done in two layers. On the project’s computer, R scripts read the complete data and produce aggregated tables. The script analisis/exportar_agregados.R transfers those tables to the book and checks that none of them identifies a child, a teacher or a school. The book is built only from those tables.
