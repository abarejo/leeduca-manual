Psychometric method

Status: first draft (November 2, 2026) of chapter 5 of the manual, expanded on November 8, 2026 with the watch zone and links to From the lab to the manual (in Spanish). It brings together in one place how tasks are scored, how reliability and validity are estimated, how norms are built and how to decide whether a change matters. Each section states what is done, which study on the group’s research agenda (in Spanish) develops it and what is missing. How each task sheet is put together is described in How each task sheet is built, and decisions already taken in Methodological decisions. Translated from the Spanish version of November 27, 2026.

In one sentence. Each task is analyzed with the method that fits the way it measures (not all with α), each score is reported with its margin of error, and each use the manual proposes (screening, monitoring, identification) rests on specific evidence (American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, and National Council on Measurement in Education 2014; Kane 2013).

5.1 Scores

One main measure per task, according to its family. Tasks are grouped into seven families according to how they measure: accuracy, rapid naming, oral reading fluency, time-limited efficiency, decision with response time, span and memory, and comprehension. The family determines the main measure, its transformation and the reliability method (see How tasks are grouped).

Higher is better. In the manual all scores are presented so that a high value is good and a low percentile indicates difficulty. Times are converted into rates (stimuli per second, words per minute).

Understandable units. Where possible, the score is also given in a unit a teacher understands: words per minute, minutes to read 1,000 words, or “reads like a child of…” (see Norms by reading level).

Session validity. Item-level times make it possible to detect an anomalous administration: times of zero, different units, interruptions, responses that are too fast, or results inconsistent with the same child at other time points. A first set of rules fixed in advance has been tested on the rapid naming tasks. The proposal is that each score carry a reliable session or check flag.

  • Missing: extend the detectors to all tasks and decide the thresholds.

5.2 Reliability

The method depends on the family. Internal consistency (α, ω) only makes sense in accuracy tasks without a time limit. In speeded tasks α overestimates reliability, and test–retest or parallel forms are appropriate.

Family Main reliability Complement
Accuracy, comprehension, span ω (or KR-20) from item responses Test–retest; IRT and conditional standard error
Rapid naming, reading fluency Test–retest and parallel forms Reliability across cards or lists
Time-limited, decision Test–retest; split-half by time Joint model of speed and accuracy

What is available today. Of the 110 assessment tasks in the catalog, 55 have a 2021 α and 33 have stability across successive administrations (ρ); 64 have at least one of the two and 46 have no reliability data yet. Stability mixes measurement error and real change, so it is a lower bound.

Standard error and bands. Each percentile comes with its 90% confidence band, computed with the standard error of measurement. With IRT, the error is conditional: wider at the extremes, where risk decisions are made.

What a reliability of .85 implies. A child whose true level is at P20 may appear not at risk in about 4 of every 10 assessments. The interactive simulation is in The dancing percentile (in Spanish). That is why reports use a watch zone between P20 and P30, in which no label is given until the next assessment (see The watch zone (in Spanish)).

  • Missing: ω and IRT with the item-level responses of the new platform for all class A and B tasks; short-term test–retest in the speeded tasks. This is what could change the most quality classes.

The criterion. Reading difficulty is defined with the child’s whole trajectory from age 6 to 11, in certainty levels (persistent, transient, typical…), not with a single assessment. This makes the criterion more stable and reduces spectrum bias. When the criterion contains the task itself, the AUC is presented only as agreement, not as predictive validity.

Beyond the cut-off. The manual proposes giving probabilities and not only a cut-off: likelihood ratios by score band and posterior probability according to the school’s prevalence. The first analyses show that a well-calibrated Bayesian model outperforms the single cut-off, and that multiplying likelihood ratios overestimates risk when indicators are correlated.

  • Missing: confirmatory factor analysis by age, invariance by sex and format, and group agreement on the certainty levels and the threshold for severe difficulty (P10 vs. −1.5 SD).

5.4 Norms

The reference sample. Norms describe development without intervention: the reference is the children from schools that did not implement Preleo with their cohort. All equivalent cohorts are pooled, unless one departs by more than 1 SD (see Methodological decisions).

By time point, not only by school year. With three assessments per school year, a single norm per school year flags almost a quarter of the children as “at risk” in the first term. That is why norms are built by school year and term, and from age 4 to 6 they are refined by age in months (see Norms by school year or by term).

Continuous norms. The aim is to replace the empirical tables with continuous norms by age (cNORM or GAMLSS), which smooth the percentiles, allow norming at any exact age and use all cohorts at once.

  • Missing: continuous norms for class A tasks, separate tables for Preleo schools, and norms for age 3 and for the versions in other languages.

5.5 How much change matters

In a monitoring system, the key question is not only where a child stands, but whether they have really progressed and whether that progress matters. The manual answers with three complementary indicators:

  1. Reliable change index: whether the difference between two assessments exceeds what is expected from measurement error. Already in the letters and speed task sheets.
  2. Growth percentile: how much a child has progressed compared with those who started at the same level.
  3. Minimal important change: how much change matters, anchored in external criteria (reading prognosis, teacher judgment, the shift from transient to persistent difficulty) (Anvari and Lakens 2021).

Beware of regression to the mean. Among children flagged as at risk, some “leave” risk at the next assessment purely because of measurement error: with a reliability of .85, more than one in four without having changed (see Leaving risk is not always improving (in Spanish)). That is why leaving risk is confirmed with another assessment.

  • Missing: settle the anchors and apply the three indicators to the class A reading tasks.

5.6 Fairness

A fair task measures the same thing and makes the same errors in all groups. This is checked in two ways: differential functioning of items and scores (sex, school socioeconomic status, format, region) and differential prediction, that is, whether the same cut-off is equally accurate in all groups. The letters task sheet has already shown that, at the same score, children from low-SES schools are at greater risk; for that reason the cut-off is not adjusted for socioeconomic status.

  • Missing: false-positive rates by group in the screening tasks.

Reproducibility

All computations are done with R scripts on the project’s computer, which read the complete data and export only aggregated tables. The book is built from those tables, and the code is in the repository. Before confirmatory studies, the analysis plan is preregistered (see How each task sheet is built).
