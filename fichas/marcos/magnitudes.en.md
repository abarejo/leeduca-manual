Comparing quantities measures **numerical magnitude processing**. In the **non-symbolic** version, the child sees two sets of dots and says whether they have the same number or not: this is attributed to the approximate number system, which estimates quantities without counting. In the **symbolic** version, the child compares two digits and chooses the larger: this requires accessing magnitude from the learned symbol.

**What the evidence says:**

- **Both are related to mathematics, the symbolic one somewhat more.** A meta-analysis of 45 articles (284 effects, 17,201 participants) estimates r = .30 for symbolic comparison and .24 for non-symbolic comparison. The relation is stronger with mental calculation and early mathematics [@schneider2017associations].
- **Symbolic knowledge better predicts arithmetic progress.** In 6-year-olds followed for 11 months, knowledge of Arabic numerals predicted arithmetic growth and magnitude comparison added nothing [@gobel2014arithmetic]. In 62 Spanish 4-year-olds, digits and arithmetic predicted later improvement of the approximate system, and not the other way round [@munez2024interrelations].
- **The dot task is not a pure measure.** Most children compare the sets by their visual features (size, area) more than by number [@clayton2019nonsymbolic].

The evidence for the approximate system as a causal basis of arithmetic is contradictory. Symbolic comparison is the strongest candidate for school screening.

**What our data show.**

- **Reliability.** The non-symbolic version has modest consistency (α = .63) and little stability across terms (ρ = .29–.38); the symbolic version, even less (ρ = .20–.21). At age 5, 25% of children reach the maximum in the non-symbolic version.
- **Validity against mathematics.** They predict low arithmetic at ages 10–11 poorly (AUC .54–.61). Symbolic comparison at ages 10–11 correlates with arithmetic at the same time point (r = .32–.39) ([Numerical cognition](../../cognicion-numerica.en.qmd#sec-mate-criterio)).
- **Validity against reading.** They are related to low Woodcock-Muñoz fluency as much as or more than to arithmetic (AUC .57–.70 for concurrent symbolic comparison; up to .77 for non-symbolic at age 5, with only 22 cases). They may reflect speed or attention more than number sense.

The grouped codes are not homogeneous: the non-symbolic task mixes an examiner-administered version (1744) and a self-administered one (10752), and code 1745 is a number line task, not a comparison task.
