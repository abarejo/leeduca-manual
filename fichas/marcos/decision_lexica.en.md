In lexical decision the child sees a string of letters and decides whether it is a word ("Is this a word?", YES/NO). It measures **visual word recognition**: fast and secure access to the orthographic lexicon. In 2021/22 it was administered to groups, on tablets, in subtests of 45 stimuli of one to four syllables. Some are biased toward words (36 words and 9 pseudowords) and others toward pseudowords (the reverse proportion). The score is efficiency: correct responses per unit of time.

**What the evidence says:**

- **Children decide by searching their lexicon, not by reading to themselves.** Beginning and advanced readers show length effects, but their orthographic neighborhood effects resemble those of adults and do not change when articulation is blocked. They base the decision on a lexical search [@vandenboer2012lexical].
- **In Spanish, the orthographic lexicon carries weight beyond decoding.** In 1,070 schoolchildren from Grade 2 to Grade 6, lexical orthographic processing contributed to reading competence and became more refined with years of practice [@cuadro2014lexico].
- **The proportion of words and pseudowords shifts the response criterion.** Participants adopt a single criterion that depends on the set of stimuli, not on each trial [@paul1992criterio]. That proportion changes both accuracy and response times [@ratcliff2004diffusion]. With 80% words, a child who always answers "YES" gets 80% right: sensitivity and bias should be separated.
- **Group screening on tablets is feasible.** A tablet application given to 616 five-year-olds predicted Grade 1 reading difficulties with a sensitivity of 90% [@zugarramurdi2022lexiland].

**What our data show.** There is one administration per child and school year: there is no stability estimate and no α.

- **Validity.** It is the family with the best concurrent validity on the platform. Against persistent difficulty, AUC from .75 to .87 between ages 9 and 11. Against low Woodcock-Muñoz fluency in the same school year, AUC from .66 to .81 (ρ = .30–.58).
- **Bias and length.** The versions biased toward words and toward pseudowords perform almost the same. The one-syllable versions, given only at ages 6–7, are the weakest (AUC .56–.74).
- **Homographs.** AUC .84–.85 against persistent difficulty and ρ = .59–.69 with Woodcock-Muñoz, the highest relation in the group. Given the instructions, it seems to measure silent sentence reading efficiency more than comprehension of homographs.
- **Caution.** At ages 6–7 efficiency is very skewed (extreme values up to 172), and at age 11 the reference group is small (80–102 children).
