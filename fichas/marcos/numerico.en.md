Counting forwards and backwards, and reading Arabic numerals, measure **symbolic number knowledge**: the verbal sequence of numerals and its correspondence with digits. This is learned knowledge, distinct from the approximate number sense (comparing quantities), and it forms the basis of school arithmetic.

**What the evidence says:**

- **Counting in preschool predicts mathematics in the first school years.** Mastery of counting principles at age 5 predicts the risk of mathematical difficulties in Grade 1 [@stock2009markers]. In Spanish children, the verbal number sequence (procedural counting) was the preschool task with the most weight in Grade 2 mathematics achievement [@mercader2017basicas].
- **Knowing Arabic numerals is one of the two pillars of early arithmetic.** In 569 children, only number knowledge and quantity discrimination uniquely predicted arithmetic progress in the first school year [@malone2020number]. Children with mathematical difficulties already show symbolic deficits in preschool, and those deficits persist [@desoete2012comparison].
- **Counting backwards is more demanding** than counting forwards. It requires retrieving the sequence in reverse order and holding it in working memory, which is why it discriminates better at age 5.

**What our data show.** The four tasks have high internal consistency (α = .84–.95), but they were given in one or two sessions and there is no stability between terms. They predict later reading fluency poorly (AUC .53–.59), which supports their discriminant validity. Against low arithmetic at ages 10–11, counting backwards in the second ten is the most specific: AUC .67 vs. .51 for reading ([Numerical cognition](../../cognicion-numerica.en.qmd#sec-mate-criterio)).
