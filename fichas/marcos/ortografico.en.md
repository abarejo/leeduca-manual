Choosing, between two forms that sound the same, the correctly spelled word (for example, *vaca* vs. *baca*) measures **orthographic knowledge**: the representations of specific words in the mental lexicon. This is what allows a word to be recognized at a glance, without converting it letter by letter.

**How it is formed.** According to the self-teaching hypothesis, each time the child successfully decodes a new word, something of its written form is stored. Phonological decoding works as an "internal teacher" [@jorm1983recoding]. Computational models show that this mechanism is enough to learn thousands of words and that initial phonological deficits are enough to produce dyslexia [@ziegler2014selfteaching]. A systematic review of 62 experiments confirms the role of phonological recoding in all writing systems [@li2022selfteaching]. It also works in Spanish, except with infrequent spellings of a sound, which need more exposure [@ferroni2014recodificacion].

**What the task measures.** Orthographic choice captures both knowledge of specific words and more general orthographic knowledge. Choosing correctly does not depend only on being able to read the word [@compton2020choice]. In Spanish, the orthographic lexicon contributes to reading competence beyond decoding, and it is refined with years of practice [@cuadro2014lexico].

**What our data show.** 2021 α = .80 and stability between terms ρ = .52, lower than for reading aloud. Even so, it is one of the tasks that best predict low reading fluency years later (median AUC .76, maximum .80). This is to be expected if it measures how many words the child has consolidated, which is precisely what fails in dyslexia in a transparent orthography.
